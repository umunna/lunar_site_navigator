## 1. What we build

A static web app for comparing lunar landing sites and dates. Pick a site (from the catalog, or any lat/lon) and a date range. It shows where the Sun and Earth are against the real local horizon, how long until sunset or Earthset, and how the coming lunar days compare. A compare view and a "what if the launch slips" tool cover the schedule-slip case in the challenge.

- **Data:** JPL Horizons for the Sun and Earth directions (3 years, 10-minute steps) and LRO LOLA for terrain (one skyline per site). Both are NASA.
- **No hardware. No server at runtime.** Horizons is called only when we build the data files.
- **Everything in the planner is geometry:** Sun or Earth above the local horizon. That is not usable power and not a working link (4.2).
- **A ladder page inside the app:** "Where am I?" simulates a walker with boot sensors, asks the planner when the Sun or Earth can correct heading, and shows how markers and pings shrink the error. Simulated, with assumed parameters, labelled as such (5.10). Real boot sensors are the next step (section 8).

One line for the pitch: *pick a site and a date, see when you have light and when you can see Earth, and what happens to both if the launch slips a lunar day.*

### 4.2 What we do not say

- Sun above the horizon is usable power. We have no array, thermal or battery model.
- Earth above the horizon is a working link. We have no DSN station visibility, antenna pointing, link budget or scheduling.
- A catalog point is the landing site. Region and feature centres are not landing sites. The `precision` field says which one it is.
- The skyline is exact. It is limited by the DEM's effective resolution, by the DEM window (the DEM stops at 80°S), and by taking the skyline at the disc centre (the Sun is about 0.5° wide, terrain can vary inside that).
- Sites outside the DEM have terrain. They get a flat horizon, and the app says so.
- Our numbers reproduce NASA's illumination maps. Not until V3 and V4 are run (5.7).
- We have a 3D view.
- The ladder page predicts real navigation accuracy. It is a simulation with assumed parameters, and no boot sensor exists. Shadow Walkers is a concept. We have no measured accuracy for step counting, markers, SLAM or UWB.
- NASA endorsement or flight readiness.
- Teammate hardware specs (for example the Livox Mid-360 IMU). Unverified `[?]`.

## 5. The demo spec (software only)

### 5.1 What a judge sees in 90 seconds

1. Open the link. A catalog site is already loaded: panorama with the terrain silhouette, Sun and Earth discs, and three readouts: Sun elevation, time to sunset, time to Earthset.
2. Drag the timeline across a lunar day. The Sun skims the horizon, ridges hide it and release it, and Earth drifts in and out.
3. Add two more sites. Stacked strips and a metric table show which site has the longest dark spell and the longest Earth gap.
4. Press "slip the launch by one lunar day". The metrics change. The heatmap marks the better dates.
5. Type custom coordinates. Sun and Earth update at once. The terrain label says what is modelled.
6. Open "Where am I?": the lander at the origin, a walk drawn on the map, simulated boot sensors, an error circle that grows. Pick a site and time where the Sun and Earth are both down: no heading fix is possible. Switch to a lit time: Sun fixes shrink the error. Drop a marker, trigger a ping, press walk-back and read the share of runs that end within 300 m of the lander.
7. Open About: validation numbers, sources, limits, AI-use statement.

### 5.3 Definitions (code and slides must agree)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Time | UTC. One sample every 600 s from `t0`. Between samples the Sun and Earth vectors are interpolated linearly. Interpolation error for the Sun is about 1.6e-5° `[calc]`: Sun moves 360° / 29.53 d = 0.085° per 10 min, error ≈ Δθ² / 8 |
| Site | Planetocentric latitude, east longitude, on a sphere of 1,737,400 m (same as the DEM's CRS `[tested]`). Observer height `h_obs` above the DEM surface: 2 m default `[design]` |
| Azimuth, elevation | Azimuth clockwise from local north. Elevation above the local horizontal plane |
| Skyline(az) | Elevation of the terrain horizon in direction `az`, including the dip below horizontal caused by the sphere and observer height. 720 bins of 0.5°, linear interpolation. 2 m observer on a smooth sphere: −0.087° `[calc]` |
| Disc radius | Sun: asin(695,700 km / distance), about 0.27° (695,700 km is the IAU nominal solar radius). Earth: asin(6,371 km / distance), about 0.95° at 384,400 km `[calc]`. Both from the sample's distance `[design]` |
| Visible fraction `f` | Share of the disc above the skyline (circular-segment area, formula in 5.6). 0 = hidden, 1 = fully visible |
| Lit / Earth visible | `f > 0`: any part of the disc above the skyline. Same rule as PGDA's Earth visibility `[✔]`. Option: disc centre above the skyline |
| Sunset, Earthset | Moment `f` falls to 0 (upper limb touches the skyline). Sunrise, Earthrise: `f` rises above 0. Crossing time by linear interpolation inside the 10-minute step |
| Time remaining | Event time minus the selected time, shown as days, hours, minutes. If there is no event before the data ends, the app says "none before <date>" |
| Window | Interval `[a, b]`. Default 14 days `[design]` |
| Window metrics | Lit %. Longest lit run. Longest dark run. Earth-visible %. Longest Earth outage. Both visible %. Neither %. Longest "neither" run. Maximum Sun elevation. Mean visible fraction (comparable in kind to PGDA's average illumination) |

"Neither" windows (no Sun and no Earth above the skyline) are where a Sun compass and direct-to-Earth contact are both unavailable `[calc from the definitions]`. That ties the planner to the ladder page (5.10).

### 5.5 Data pipeline (build time, Node and TypeScript)

**P1 `fetch-ephemeris`.** The method validated on 14 Nov `[tested]`:

- Horizons request: `COMMAND='301'` (Moon), `CENTER='500@399'` (Earth centre), `QUANTITIES='14,15,19,20'`, `STEP_SIZE='10 min'`, `CSV_FORMAT='YES'`, `RANGE_UNITS='KM'`.
- These give the sub-Earth and sub-solar longitude and latitude in the Moon's body-fixed frame, plus the Sun and Earth distances. Unit vector from lon/lat times distance gives the Sun and Earth vectors.
- One request per year (under the 90,000-row cap per request `[✔]`). A response with an input error can still be HTTP 200, so check for `$$SOE`. Future UTC assumes the last known leap second `[✔]`.
- Do not use `VECTORS` with `REF_PLANE='BODY'`: it is inertial.
- Output `ephemeris.bin`: a small header (magic, version, `t0`, step, count, source note), then per step 6 × Float32: Sun x, y, z and Earth x, y, z in km. About 157,800 steps × 24 B = 3.8 MB `[calc]`. Float32 gives about 3e-6° direction error `[calc]`. V1 checks it.
- Untested: 52,000-row requests (only 121 rows tested). Fallback: quarterly chunks. Cache raw responses on disk.

**P2 `catalog.ts`** (typed dictionary). Fields: `id`, `name`, `group` (artemis-region, apollo, clps, other, custom), `lat`, `lon`, `source`, `precision` (`landing-site`, `feature-centre`, `region-centre`, `user`), `notes`. Coordinates are looked up from a cited source (IAU Gazetteer, NASA mission pages, LROC), never typed from memory.

**P3 `build-skylines`.** One skyline per catalog site inside the DEM, algorithm in 5.6. Output about 2.9 KB per site and observer height (720 × Float32), plus a flag for "window truncated at the DEM edge" (sites within about 60 km of the 80°S circle, for example north of 82°S `[calc]`).

**P4 `validate`.** Runs V1 to V4, writes `VALIDATION.md` and the data shown on the About page.

### 5.6 Maths

**Sky position of a body (Sun or Earth) from a site:**

```
up    = (cosφ cosλ, cosφ sinλ, sinφ)
east  = (−sinλ, cosλ, 0)
north = (−sinφ cosλ, −sinφ sinλ, cosφ)
S     = (R + h) · up                    site vector, R = 1,737.4 km
d     = B − S                           B = Sun or Earth vector from the ephemeris
el    = asin( (d · up) / |d| )
az    = atan2( d · east, d · north )  mod 360      clockwise from north
```

The validated script used `S = R · up` (no `h`). The difference is at most 0.001° `[calc: 7 km of height / 384,400 km]`.

**Skyline from the DEM** (exact on a sphere, per azimuth `α`, 720 bins):

```
R_o = R + h_site + h_obs                observer radius, h_site from the DEM
for d = step … until stop:
    θ  = d / R
    P  = point at arc θ, bearing α from the site (great-circle formula)
    h_t = DEM(P) by bilinear interpolation   (P to DEM x, y through the projection above)
    ε   = atan2( (R + h_t)·cos θ − R_o , (R + h_t)·sin θ )
skyline[α] = max ε
stop when the highest possible terrain (DEM maximum, needs R + h_max ≥ R_o) at distance d cannot beat the max so far,
or P leaves the 80°S circle (mark the skyline "truncated")
```

`step` = DEM pixel size (80 m or 20 m), finer near the sphere's tangent distance. Cost is small: 720 bearings × a few thousand samples per site `[calc]`.

**Visible fraction of a disc** (radius `r`, centre elevation `el`, skyline `s`):

```
x = (s − el) / r             clipped to [−1, 1]
f = ( acos(x) − x·sqrt(1 − x²) ) / π
```

`f` = 0.5 when the centre sits on the skyline, 1 when the disc is fully above, 0 when fully below. Lit means `f > 0`. Event times come from `g = el + r − s` crossing 0.

### 5.10 The ladder demo (screen 5, simulated)

**What it is.** A 2D page inside the app that shows how a walker keeps track of where they are when the usual cues fail. The lander sits at (0, 0). The user clicks out a path or picks a preset (out-and-back, loop, wedge). A simulated boot-sensor stream gives step events and a heading. The page dead-reckons the position, draws the walked path as the boots would map it, and shows how uncertain the position is. The plane is flat: no terrain, no obstacles. **All sensor behaviour is simulated with assumed parameters. No boot sensor exists yet (section 8).**

**Tie to the planner.** A Sun or Earth sighting can correct heading only while that body is above the skyline. NASA's paper lists aligning a map with the Sun, Earth or the lander, and a Sun compass, as heading methods `[✔]`. Apollo set the rover gyro from the Sun using ephemeris Sun azimuth and elevation `[✔]`. The page asks the planner, for the chosen site and the simulated clock (start time plus walking time), whether the Sun or Earth is up. If neither is, no heading fix is possible and the error grows. That is the "neither" window of 5.3 made visible.

**Model** (pure TypeScript in `packages/shared`, deterministic for a given seed):

1. True path: waypoints walked at constant speed.
2. Boot sensor (simulated): one step event per stride. Stride length has a scale error and per-step noise. Heading comes from a gyro with an initial error and a drift rate.
3. Estimate: the sum of strides along the measured heading (dead reckoning).
4. Error circle: 200 runs on the same true path with fresh noise. The page shows run 0 as the walker's estimate. The circle is centred on it, with a radius that holds 95% of the 200 runs' position errors. It is a simulated spread, not an analytic bound.
5. Fixes, each switchable:
   - Sun or Earth heading fix, while the planner says the body is up: heading is reset to the true heading plus noise.
   - Marker: dropped at the current estimate. Passing within the detection radius resets the estimate to the stored one, plus detection noise. A marker carries the error it had when dropped, so it shrinks the error to that earlier level, not to zero.
   - Lander ping: inside the ping range, position is reset to the true position plus noise.
6. Walk-back: from each run's estimated position, command the straight bearing and distance to the lander, walk it with the same noise, and report the share of runs that end within 300 m of the lander, the distance from which NASA's analysis assumes the lander is visible `[✔]`.
7. Overlays: estimated path and error circle always; true path only in a "teaching" view (a real walker never knows it); markers; a 150 m circle and a 10° heading line (NASA's threshold for a walk-back of about 1.2 to 1.4 km, under the paper's assumptions `[✔]`).
8. Export: the trail CSV (appendix A).

**Parameters.** The sliders start here. Everything marked "assumed" is labelled "assumed" on screen.

| Parameter | Default | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Walking speed | 2 km/h | NASA's walk-back analysis assumption `[✔]` |
| Lander visible from | 300 m | NASA's walk-back analysis assumption `[✔]` |
| Threshold | 150 m and 10° | NASA's "threshold performance navigation requirement" `[✔]` |
| Heading drift | 10°/h | Apollo's gyro requirement for lunar operation was under 10°/h `[✔]`. A requirement, not a measured drift. Used as a scale. Assumed for boots |
| Sun heading-fix noise | 2° (1σ) | Assumed. For context, the Apollo report's requirements table lists ±2° (rss, 3σ system accuracy) for the Sun shadow device `[✔]` |
| Stride length, scale error, per-step noise | 0.7 m, ±2%, 5% | Assumed |
| Initial heading error | 1° | Assumed |
| Marker detection radius | 5 m | Assumed |
| Ping range, ping noise | 300 m, 5 m (1σ) | Assumed |
| Runs per scenario | 200 | `[design]` |

## 9. Appendix A: the fallback ladder (simulated in the app, hardware not built)

Use the top rung that still works. When its input dies, drop one rung. A site with long "neither" windows (5.3) is where the lower rungs matter.

| Rung | What it is | Needs | Stops working when | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Orbiter or lander fix: absolute position from a link; the ping carries position, time and uncertainty | Line of sight | A crater wall blocks it, Earth is below the horizon, or the orbiter service has gaps | NASA: terrain blocks RF line of sight; orbiter service "might not be continuous" `[✔]` |
| 2 | LiDAR + IMU SLAM, suit-to-suit ranging, map merging (Shadow Walkers) | Power, compute, partner in range | Battery or compute fails, partner out of range | NASA: LiDAR works in any light but costs more power, mass and compute `[✔]` |
| 3 | IMU or gyro dead reckoning, heading set before entering the dark | Power | Drift grows with time | Apollo rover gyro and odometers: maximum position errors under about 100 m (Apollo 16, 17) to under about 350 m (Apollo 15) on traverses up to 7.6 km from the lander `[✔]` |
| 4 | Step count + Sun or Earth compass | Legs; Sun or Earth in view | Both hidden; stride length changes `[?]` | NASA lists counting steps for position, and Sun and Earth direction for heading `[✔]` |
| 5 | Markers + automatic log | Markers in hand | You run out or miss one | NASA studies beacons and optical markers `[✔]` |
| 6 | Own footprints, retraced | A headlamp | Trail scuffed or crossed `[?]` | LRO still shows the Apollo 14 walk from orbit `[✔]` |

**First principles behind it:**

- At sea: dead reckoning, an error circle that grows, a fix that resets it, a log plotted at least hourly `[✔]`. Latitude from the Sun's height at noon. Longitude needs an accurate clock: 4 minutes of time is 1° `[✔]`.
- On the Moon: no global magnetic field `[✔]`, so heading comes from the Sun, Earth, stars or a gyro. Apollo set the rover gyro with the Sun shadow device and an ephemeris `[✔]`. Apollo 16's rover range and bearing froze on one traverse after pulses from two front wheels were lost (cause inferred by the report) `[✔]`. Hence fallbacks.

**Boot sensors, the hardware step (roadmap, section 8).** The ladder page's simulated sensor stream is meant to be replaced by real logs from sensors in the boots: step events and heading from a foot-mounted inertial unit, mapped as a path. Foot-mounted inertial navigation with zero-velocity updates is an established technique. A 2020 review says zero-velocity updates break the cubic position-error growth of stand-alone inertial navigation, but position, yaw and the gyro bias along gravity stay unobservable, so position and yaw errors gradually increase `[✔]`. That is why the ladder needs heading fixes (Sun, Earth), markers and pings. NASA's paper lists counting steps, pedometers, accelerometers and IMUs for position from a start `[✔]`. Draper reports field tests of a wearable camera and IMU system for astronauts (not boot-mounted); its abstract says drift "can be reduced to a rate of ~0.5-1% of distance traveled" and gives no quantitative test results `[✔]`. Not known for the Moon: stride behaviour in a pressurised suit in one-sixth gravity, and the effect of dust, cold and vacuum on boot hardware `[?]`.

**One shared trail format** (the ladder page exports it; real boot logs and a later SLAM simulation would use it too): CSV with `t_s`, `x_m`, `y_m`, `sigma_m`, `source` (`step`, `imu`, `slam`, `marker`, `uwb`, `lander-ping`). The return path is the shortest route back over the trail.

**One chart** (later): position uncertainty against distance walked, one line per rung, with NASA's 150 m threshold drawn. Label every line "real (Apollo)", "simulated" or "assumed". Never mix them.
